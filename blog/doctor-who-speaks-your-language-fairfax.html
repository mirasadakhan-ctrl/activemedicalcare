Seeing a doctor who speaks your language in Fairfax

Care in English, Hindi, Urdu and Telugu at Active Medical Care. Learn how to prepare questions and discuss your care clearly.

Published October 8, 2026 · General health information

A medical appointment involves more than recognizing a few familiar words. You need room to explain what concerns you, ask about unfamiliar terms and understand what comes next. For people who prefer Hindi, Urdu or Telugu, speaking directly with a doctor in that language can make the conversation more comfortable. At Active Medical Care in Fairfax, Dr. Nisa Masood, MD, offers care in English, Hindi, Urdu and Telugu.

Tell the office your preferred language

Mention your language preference when you call rather than waiting until the appointment begins. Explain which language you would like to use for the conversation and whether you need help with written information. Speaking a language and having every form available in that language are different things, so ask what the office can provide. This helps the team prepare and gives you a chance to discuss any access needs in advance. A preference can also change from one visit to another depending on the topic and who is joining you.

Prepare the story you want to tell

Before the visit, write down what you want the doctor to understand. You might include when a concern started, what has changed and which question matters most to you. Use the language you find easiest; the list is there to help you remember, not to test your medical vocabulary. Bring medicine names or containers as well as your written description. Exact names can be useful even when the rest of the conversation takes place in another language. If you are uncertain about a term, say so instead of guessing.

Ask for plain explanations

Medical terms can be confusing in any language. Ask the doctor to explain an unfamiliar word, why a test is recommended or what a medicine instruction means. You can ask for one step at a time if several instructions arrive together. Before leaving, describe the plan in your own words and ask whether you have understood it correctly. This is a practical way to catch a misunderstanding while you can still discuss it. You do not need to feel embarrassed about asking a question twice or requesting a slower explanation.

Keep language needs separate from assumptions

People who speak the same language do not all share the same experiences, preferences or beliefs. Tell the doctor what matters to you rather than assuming it will be obvious. This may include routines, food preferences, work schedules or the role you would like family members to have in appointments. A respectful conversation makes space for those details without treating language as a diagnosis or a complete description of a person. You can choose the language that helps you communicate clearly even if you are comfortable using English in other settings.

Discuss family involvement and privacy

A relative or friend may help you remember questions or organize records, but you can also ask to speak privately with the doctor. Tell the office who you would like involved and what help you want from them. For situations requiring interpretation, ask about appropriate language assistance rather than relying automatically on a child or an untrained person to explain medical information. HHS emphasizes effective communication in healthcare. The right arrangement should support understanding and respect your privacy; it should not make you feel unable to ask a sensitive question.

Confirm the written next steps

Before leaving, ask how you will receive results, instructions or follow-up information. If a document contains words you do not understand, ask the team to explain them and clarify whether a translated version or other assistance is available. Do not assume a phone translation tool can safely resolve every medical instruction. Keep the contact number and follow-up date somewhere easy to find. If you realize later that an instruction is unclear, call the office. It is better to check than to make a guess about a test, medicine or appointment.
