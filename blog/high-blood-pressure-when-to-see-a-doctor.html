High blood pressure: when to see a doctor and what to expect

Learn how to prepare for a blood pressure follow-up, discuss home readings and recognize situations that need urgent care.

Published October 8, 2026 · General health information

A high blood pressure reading can leave you with questions: was it a one-time result, should you check again, and what should you bring to an appointment? High blood pressure often has no obvious symptoms, so follow-up should be based on readings and medical advice rather than how well you feel. This guide explains how to prepare for a primary care discussion in Fairfax.

A reading needs context

Blood pressure can vary, and one measurement is not the whole medical picture. Your clinician considers repeated readings, your health history, medicines and other factors when deciding what a result means. You may be asked to have additional measurements or keep a home record. Avoid diagnosing yourself from one number or comparing your treatment with someone else's. If another clinician, pharmacy or workplace screening advised you to follow up, bring that information and the original reading to the appointment.

Arrange follow-up rather than waiting for symptoms

Feeling well does not rule out high blood pressure. If readings have repeatedly been high, or a clinician has asked you to return, call to arrange the recommended follow-up. Tell the office when the reading was taken, what it was and whether you have symptoms, so the team can advise on the appropriate next step. Website information cannot decide the urgency of your individual situation. If you are unsure whether a concern can wait, contact a clinician rather than waiting for a routine appointment on your own.

Know when the situation is urgent

Very high blood pressure readings require prompt medical attention. NHLBI identifies readings above 180 systolic or above 120 diastolic as dangerously high. If a high reading is accompanied by chest pain, severe breathing trouble, sudden weakness, difficulty speaking or a sudden change in vision, call 911. Do not wait for an office or video appointment, and do not wait for symptoms to pass on their own. If you have a very high reading without those symptoms, seek immediate advice from a healthcare professional about what to do next.

Bring a useful home record

If your care team has asked you to monitor at home, bring the readings with dates and times rather than only your highest or lowest result. Note questions about the device, cuff or instructions you were given. Ask whether you should bring the monitor so the office can discuss technique. The record is a tool for a conversation, not a reason to change medicine doses yourself. Follow the monitoring schedule recommended for you and ask what to do if readings fall outside the range your clinician has discussed.

Expect a review of more than the numbers

The doctor may ask about your medical and family history, current medicines, sleep, activity and other aspects of daily life. Bring a list of prescription and nonprescription products, vitamins and supplements. Mention side effects, missed doses, refill difficulties or costs that make a plan hard to follow. These details can be relevant to the discussion. Depending on your needs, the doctor may recommend tests, further measurements or referral. Ask why each next step is recommended and how it will help guide your care.

Make the follow-up plan practical

Before leaving, ask when to return, how to share home readings and whom to call about questions between visits. If a medicine is prescribed or changed, clarify the instructions with the clinician or pharmacist. Do not stop it because you feel well or because of an online article. Tell the team if part of the plan is difficult to carry out. A useful plan is one you understand, and asking the same question again is reasonable when an answer was rushed or unfamiliar.

Frequently asked questions

Can I have high blood pressure without feeling ill?

Yes. High blood pressure often has no obvious symptoms. Follow recommended measurements and appointments even when you feel well.

Does one high reading confirm a diagnosis?

Not necessarily. A clinician considers repeat measurements and your medical history. Bring the reading and any advice you received.

Should I change my medicines after a high reading?

Do not change doses on your own. Contact your clinician for instructions; seek emergency help for severe or sudden symptoms.
